WardZero · Product requirements and roadmap · draft 3 · 3 Nov 2026

WardZero Persona Agents

Simulated hospital visitors and patients who have a body, a persona and a memory. They live in the Isaac Sim hospital beside a Unitree G1, react to what it says and does, and give us a repeatable way to test the robot on tasks we have not written yet.

Status draft, awaiting approval Version in scope v1 (waiting area, 5 people) Target hardware RTX 5090 32 GB (dev on RTX 3070 8 GB) Robot middleware ROS 2 Humble, CycloneDDS Design spec docs/design/2026-11-03-human-persona-agents-design.md

01Summary

Each simulated person has three layers. A mind is a LangGraph agent on Claude or OpenAI that decides what to do next from the person's persona, what they see and hear, and what they remember. Reflexes are fast rules with no AI that keep the person alive between decisions: coughing, fidgeting, turning towards a voice. The body is a physically simulated SOMA-23 human that follows the chosen behaviour with real balance and contact, using the chair-trained tracker we already have.

ROS 2 connects the people to the robot. The robot never reads the people's minds. It hears and sees them only as its own sensors would, so any G1 task can be tested against the same crowd.

On launch, a storyboard starts the hospital day. A scenario control panel then lets the experimenter throw scenarios at the robot while the run is going: a coughing fit, a lost visitor, a name called, a fire alarm. The people play these out in character, and the robot has to respond.

Version 1 covers the main-entrance waiting area with five people, two of whom are coughing patients. It runs for up to an hour. Personas and scenarios live in files. Memory grows over time and can be reset or restored. Every run can be replayed exactly without calling an AI model again.

02Problem and users

Our scripted reception film (UC2) proved that physics humans work in the hospital: seated hip error is now 0.003–0.051 m, with no falls. But a script fixes every movement in advance. It cannot answer when the robot says something new, it does not scale to an hour, and it cannot test robot tasks nobody has scripted.

HRI researcher

  • Runs studies with controlled, varied, believable people.
  • Needs repeatable runs, known starting memory and a record of why each person acted.

Robot developer

  • Tests a new G1 behaviour (speech, navigation, assistance) against a live room.
  • Needs the same crowd for every build, replay with no API cost, and only real sensor data reaching the robot.

Scenario author

  • Adds personas and scenarios by editing files, citing the research notes.
  • Needs clear schemas and validation errors in plain words.

New user of the repo

  • Brings their own Claude or OpenAI account.
  • Needs a README that gets the services running and keeps their key safe.

03Goals, non-goals, anti-goals

Goals

  1. Five physics people sit, cough, fidget, look around, use phones, read signs, stand, walk and talk in the waiting area for up to an hour. They don't fall or pass through walls and chairs.
  2. Decisions follow each persona and the UK research notes, and change when the robot speaks or acts nearby.
  3. Every aspect of the people is configurable from files.
  4. Memory builds up within and across runs, with reset and snapshots.
  5. Each user runs on their own Claude or OpenAI account, with keys stored securely. Nothing runs without a key.
  6. A storyboard starts on launch, and a control panel lets the experimenter throw scenarios at the robot during the run.
  7. Two perception modes are chosen at launch, each with README setup steps.
  8. It runs comfortably on one RTX 5090 in the long term.

Non-goals for v1

  • Benchmarking or scoring the G1 (moved to v2).
  • A configuration screen (v2).
  • Areas beyond the waiting area; crowds larger than 5.
  • Live photoreal rendering (we render spans from the recording instead).
  • Getting up from the floor after a fall (respawn instead).

Anti-goals (failure even if it works)

  • People who freeze or glitch while waiting for an AI reply.
  • Behaviour that depends on robot internals or hidden topics.
  • API keys in git, logs, ROS parameters, topics or recordings.
  • A run that cannot be repeated exactly.

04Version 1 scope: the waiting area

The setting is the main-entrance waiting area of the WardZero hospital. It uses the existing lobby chair rows, which were measured from the mesh and rescaled to a 0.45 m seat with fabric and wood friction, and the baked hospital collision world of 2,571 meshes. The G1 runs its normal ROS 2 stack and does whatever task it is being tested on.

#PersonaBehaviour sketchGrounding in the research
P1Coughing patient AAdult, persistent cough, waits seated, sometimes checks phone, coughs into elbowCough is a top-20 A&E chief complaint (about 436k a year, ECDS 2024-25)
P2Coughing patient BOlder adult, cough with breathlessness, slow sit-to-stand, relies on P3Older patients have longer waits and lower mobility (patient personas, mobility notes)

P1 and P2 come from the user's brief. P3–P5 are proposals and still need confirming. All five are instances of personas in config/personas.yaml, so different seeds give different people.

05Storyboard and scenario control

A run is not just people waiting. When WardZero launches, a storyboard starts: a timed sequence of beats that gives the hour its shape. While it runs, the experimenter uses the scenario control panel to fire extra scenario cards. Each card is a ready-made situation that tests how the robot responds. The people act out every beat and card through their own minds, so the same card plays out differently with different personas and seeds.

Cards work through three channels: nudging a person's mind, overriding a reflex, or changing the world. None of them tell the robot anything. The robot only meets the consequences, as it would in a real hospital.

5.2 Storyboard

  • Starts on launch. hhumans run loads the scenario's storyboard and plays it from t = 0.
  • Beats fire at a time ("t+05:00"), on a condition ("robot within 2 m of P5", "P2 has waited 20 min"), or when the previous beat ends.
  • Chaos mode (optional) draws random cards at a set rate from a weighted deck, using the run seed. Two runs with the same seed get the same cards at the same times.
  • Every beat and card firing goes into the run folder: who fired it, when, and with what parameters. That keeps the run replayable.

5.4 Scenario control panel

What the panel shows

  • The storyboard timeline, with the current beat and the next ones
  • A floor map with each person's position, posture and mood
  • Each person's last decision, its reason, and their last words
  • An event log of beats, cards, speech and falls
  • A cost meter and the recording status

What the panel does

  • Start, pause, resume, stop and reset the run
  • Fire any card now, choosing the target person and parameters
  • Turn chaos mode on or off and set its rate
  • Skip or re-run a storyboard beat
  • Save a memory snapshot

The panel is for the experimenter. It can see inside the people's minds, which the robot never can. It runs locally in a browser, served by the scenario director, and every action it takes goes into the run folder. Editing personas and cards in the panel waits for v2. In v1, cards and storyboards are YAML files.

Non-functional

IDRequirementTarget
NFR-1Real-time runningFull mode holds real time on the RTX 5090. On the RTX 3070 the research estimates a real-time factor of 0.5–0.8 at 600 Hz, so it runs on sim time with --lockstep, so the hardware changes speed but not behaviour. The user accepted slower than real time on the 3070 (3 Nov).

NFR-4ReproducibilitySame seed and recorded replies give the same run.
NFR-5Cost visibilityTokens and estimated cost are logged per person and per run. The scenario file holds a budget cap that stops new AI calls (the bodies continue).
NFR-6SecurityNo secret in git, logs, ROS or the run folder. A pre-commit secret scan runs. See section 10.
NFR-7LicencesBONES-SEED and derived clips stay local. No SMPL models. Research notes keep their citations.
NFR-9Independent of any one serverEvery model, motion clip and asset is fetched by repo scripts from its original source, using the user's own accounts and licences. Licensed data is never committed.
NFR-8Fairness to the robotNo G1-stack node subscribes to /humans/*. A launch check warns if one does.

Implementation notes from the review

  • Structured output: use the API's native structured-output mode (output_config.format), not a forced tool call. Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 reject forced tool_choice, and LangChain's Anthropic structured-output helper forces one by default. If the wrapper does not pass output_config.effort, call the Anthropic SDK directly in that graph node.
  • Thinking cannot be switched off on Opus 5.5. Even at low effort, a decision may take a few seconds. Phase 1 measures Opus 5.5 (low) against Sonnet 5.5 for latency and persona quality, and sets the default from the result. Reflexes cover the gap.
  • Cache lifetime: use the 1-hour prompt cache. Decisions can be minutes apart, longer than the default 5 minutes.

Estimated cost of one hour-long oracle run (to be measured in phase 1). Assumptions:

  • 5 people making 60–120 decisions each, so up to 600 calls.
  • About 8k input tokens per call, mostly served from the cache.
  • About 300–600 output tokens per call, including thinking.

Estimates per run:

  • Claude Opus 5.5: about $10–15.
  • Claude Sonnet 5.5: about $5–8.
  • Haiku summaries: under $0.50.
  • Replays: $0.
  • Full mode adds image calls, set by how often frames are sent.

10Keys and security

  • Bring your own account. Each user needs an Anthropic Console or OpenAI Platform account with pay-as-you-go billing. Claude Pro or Max and ChatGPT subscriptions cannot be used for API calls. The README covers creating a key and setting a monthly spend limit.
  • Storage, in order of preference:
    1. ant auth login: an Anthropic OAuth profile with short-lived tokens.
    2. hhumans keys set claude|openai: stored in the OS keyring (GNOME Keyring / Secret Service).
    3. Headless servers: a key file outside the repo with mode 600, located through an environment variable.
  • One holder. Only the LLM gateway reads the key. ROS parameters and topics are readable by anyone on the DDS domain, so a key must never touch them.
  • Never written: not to git, logs, launch files, ROS, recordings or run folders. Logs redact anything that looks like a key, and a pre-commit secret scan guards the repo.
  • No key, no run. One tiny authenticated call at start-up. If it fails, the run stops and prints the setup steps.
  • Budget cap per scenario. When it is reached, new AI calls stop and the bodies carry on with their last intents and reflexes.

11Strategy review

This is a critical read of the whole approach. It asks what holds up, what is risky, and what we rejected and why.

What holds up

  • Separating mind, reflexes and body fits the timescales. Physics runs at 120 Hz, the tracker at 30 Hz, and AI decisions take seconds. Letting an AI model drive joints or even per-frame motion would be slow, expensive and unstable.
  • Event-driven decisions keep cost roughly proportional to how much happens in the room, not to the clock.
  • Keeping the robot blind to the people's minds is what makes this a fair test of any G1 task, including tasks not yet written.
  • Replay from recorded decisions fixes the biggest weakness of AI-driven agents for research, which is that they are not repeatable. It also makes regression testing of robot builds free.
  • Grounding personas in cited research through retrieval (RAG) makes behaviour defensible in a study write-up.
  • Building on what works: the chair-trained tracker, the measured chairs, the collision world, the memory-capped launcher and the photoreal renderer are all reused.

Risks

RiskLevelMitigation
The tracker stumbles when an intent switches mid-motion. It was trained on whole clips, not live stitching.HIGHSpike S1 first. Then, in order: longer blends, transition clips, and finally a short fine-tune on stitched sequences (only with the user's go-ahead).
The tracker reads a preloaded motion library and freezes on its last frame, so live composition needs a streaming motion sourceHIGHPart of spike S1: a patched or replaced motion manager serving a rolling window per person.
Shared 600 Hz physics (needed for the G1's 500 Hz-class PD). The tracker was trained at 120 Hz.MEDSpike S3 compares tracking at 120 and 600 Hz. Fallback is 300 Hz. The tick scheduler from the integration notes is used.
No trained G1 walking policy exists yetMEDv1 robot role decided by Q1. A standing robot that speaks needs no new policy.
Motion clips: the BONES-SEED SOMA archive is 45 GB (gzip, so it cannot be partly downloaded), and the PC has about 24 GB freeMEDStream the archive from Hugging Face straight through tar, keeping only the listed clips, then convert them and delete the BVH files in batches. No server is needed.
No seated or standing cough clips in BONES-SEEDMEDSearch the manifest. Otherwise use a synthetic overlay (torso and head flexion, hand or elbow to mouth), as with the nod.
AI latency spikes or rate limits make people seem slow to respondMEDReflexes acknowledge at once (head turn). The body keeps its intent. Decisions are debounced. Lockstep mode is available for studies where timing must not depend on the network.
Exact replay holds only on the same machine and buildLOWThe run folder records the GPU, driver and build. Cross-machine studies use rerun or replay --intents.
Out-of-character or unsafe repliesMEDSchema-checked output, an allow-list of intents, the persona card first in every prompt, and the reason recorded for review.
Running 5 people, the G1, the 2.7M-triangle collision world and 5 cameras on one GPUMEDOracle mode on the RTX 3070, on sim time with lockstep. Full mode on the RTX 5090, with low-rate cameras rendered on events. S3 measures the real-time factor.
Host RAM blow-up from scene objectsLOWOne person per env (5 envs, shared origin), the capped launcher, and chairs as global prims rather than per-env objects.
Memory drift: a long-term memory gathers odd beliefs over many runsLOWSnapshots, reset, importance-weighted summaries, and inspectable SQLite.

Alternatives considered

AlternativeWhy not (for now)
Isaac Sim's built-in animated people (kinematic)No physics, so no real contact with chairs or the robot, which defeats the purpose.
MaskedMimic or other goal-conditioned controllersReleased checkpoints are SMPL-based (non-commercial body model). SOMA is our body. It remains an option if a SOMA version appears.
One "director" AI controlling all five peopleCheaper, but people would share one mind, with no private perception or memory. Per-person agents match the study design.
AI decision every frame or on a fixed fast clockCost and latency explode, and nothing is gained over event triggers plus reflexes.
Custom ROS messages across the sim boundaryIsaac's bundled Python would need its own message build. JSON in standard messages is simpler for v1, and custom types can follow.
Local open-weight AI modelsThe user chose Claude or OpenAI. The gateway design leaves room for a local provider later.
VERDICT The strategy is sound, provided the motion-stitching spike passes. Everything else is integration of known parts. Run the three spikes before committing to the build plan.

15Glossary

SOMA-23The 23-body, 66-joint human skeleton used by NVIDIA's ProtoMotions trackers and BONES-SEED clips.
TrackerThe trained physics controller that makes a simulated human follow a reference motion with real balance.
IntentA behaviour the mind asks the body to perform, such as sit_down(seat_id).
Oracle modeThe simulator states what each person can see and hear, computed from the scene, with no cameras.
Full modePeople perceive through rendered head cameras and scene audio, interpreted by AI and speech-to-text models.
RAGRetrieval-augmented generation: relevant notes and memories are looked up and added to each decision prompt.
Tick schedulerThe 600 Hz physics loop that steps the people every 20 ticks (30 Hz) and the G1 every 12 ticks (50 Hz).
StoryboardThe timed sequence of beats that starts when WardZero launches and gives a run its shape.
Scenario cardA ready-made situation (a coughing fit, a lost visitor, a fire alarm) fired by the storyboard, chaos mode or the experimenter.
ReplayRe-running a recorded hour using its saved decisions instead of calling the AI provider.
